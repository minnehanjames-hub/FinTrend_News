Higher for Longer, For Real This Time

The Fed didn’t just raise rates — it retired the soft-landing script. A note on what a world of sticky inflation, 5% yields and a hawkish dot plot actually asks of investors, and the geopolitical fuse still burning underneath it.

AM
Arshia Morshedian
Macro Strategist
SUN · SEP 20 · 2026

For two years, “higher for longer” was a phrase the market said without believing. Every dip was bought on the quiet assumption that the Federal Reserve would ride to the rescue at the first sign of trouble. Wednesday ended that. By raising rates for the first time since 2023 — unanimously — and printing a dot plot in which 16 of 19 officials see even more tightening ahead, the Fed did something more consequential than a single quarter-point move. It retired the reflex. The put is gone.

That is the real content of last week, and it deserves a macro lens rather than a market one. What changes when investors finally take “higher for longer” literally? Three things move to the center of the frame: the discount rate, the geopolitical fuse under inflation, and the growing gap between what the White House wants and what the central bank will do.

1. The discount rate is the whole game now

With the funds rate at 3.75%–4.00% and the 10-year Treasury sitting at 5% — a level it had not seen since 2023 — the price of money has fundamentally reset. Every valuation in the market is a claim on future cash flows divided by a discount rate, and that denominator just got structurally larger. The assets most exposed are the ones whose payoffs sit furthest in the future: long-duration growth, unprofitable technology, and anything valued on a story about the next decade rather than the next quarter.

This is why the market’s character, not just its level, is shifting. In a 5% world, cash flows today are worth more relative to promises about tomorrow. That favors profitability over narrative, and it quietly rewards the parts of the market — financials, energy, quality cyclicals — that spent the easy-money era out of fashion. It does not mean growth stocks collapse; it means they have to earn their multiples with actual earnings rather than a falling discount rate doing the work for them.

2. The fuse is still burning under inflation

It is worth being precise about why the Fed is hiking, because it is not the usual reason. This is not an overheating-demand story — it is a supply story wearing an inflation costume. The war with Iran and the disruption to the Strait of Hormuz have kept crude near $100, and energy is bleeding straight into the headline price data. Last week that fuse got longer, not shorter: reports emerged of a potential $24 billion U.S. sale of F-35 jets to Saudi Arabia, and the President spoke openly of a “big decision” on further action against Iran even as Houthi attacks continued.

For a macro investor, this is the uncomfortable core of the regime. The Fed is tightening to defend its credibility against an inflation source it cannot control, which means the risk is asymmetric: any escalation that spikes oil forces the Fed to stay restrictive even if the domestic economy softens. That is the definition of a policy trap, and it is why the front end of the curve can stay high even as growth data wobbles.

The Fed is not fighting an overheating economy. It is fighting a shipping lane it cannot reopen — and defending the one thing it still controls, which is whether anyone believes it will hold the line.FinTrend Macro Desk

3. The politics are only going to get louder

There is a structural tension worth naming. A White House that has consistently pushed for lower rates now has a Fed, under a chair it appointed, doing the opposite — and doing it unanimously. Warsh’s challenge is that standing pat when inflation is elevated would have fed suspicion of political accommodation, while hiking invites direct conflict. He chose credibility. That choice buys the Fed independence capital now, but it sets up a running political friction that will color every meeting into 2027, and markets will increasingly have to price the noise around the institution, not just its decisions.

What it means for a portfolio

None of this is a call to hide. Growth is firm, credit is functioning, and the S&P is still up double digits on the year — a hiking cycle born of strength is very different from one fighting a recession. The macro message is subtler: this is a regime that rewards discipline over duration. It argues for owning real earnings rather than long-dated stories, for treating energy and defense-exposed geopolitics as a live portfolio variable rather than a headline, and for accepting that the “buy every dip because the Fed will save us” era is genuinely over. The dip may still be worth buying — but now you have to have a reason beyond the Fed.

The macro read

Wednesday’s hike retired the soft-landing reflex: “higher for longer” is now literal, with a 5% 10-year as the market’s new gravity and a hawkish dot plot removing the safety net. The driver is a supply-side oil shock the Fed can’t fix, and the geopolitical fuse under it — Iran, the Gulf, the F-35 sale — only lengthened last week. The regime rewards profitability over narrative and short duration over long. The Fed put isn’t paused. It’s retired.
