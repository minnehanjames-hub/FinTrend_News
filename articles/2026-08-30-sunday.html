The Hike Nobody Wanted to Price

For a year the market's base case was a patient Fed drifting toward cuts. Kevin Warsh just spent his Jackson Hole debut dismantling it. A September rate hike — into a slowing economy — is now the more likely outcome, and the curve is already bracing for it.

Arshia MorshedianMacro — The Sunday Macro Note
Sun · Aug 30, 2026

The most important thing that happened this week was a shift in a probability. Before Friday, futures markets gave a September rate hike roughly a one-in-three chance. After Kevin Warsh spoke in Jackson Hole, that number was closer to three-in-five. A hike that the market had spent months refusing to price is now its base case — and the significance runs deeper than a single meeting.

A chair who sees a boom

Warsh's framing is the key to everything. In his remarks he described the current climate not as a slowdown to be cushioned but as a period of “secular growth” and a “global investment surge” — the AI build-out pulling capital and cash flow toward business expansion. A chair who sees a boom does not cut rates into it. He guards against the inflation a boom can produce. That worldview, more than any single data point, is why the hike is now live.

The data gave him just enough cover. Core PCE held at 3.3% — stuck, not falling, and well above the 2% target. The summer's inflation prints, he said, do not convince him that underlying trends have “meaningfully improved.” He wants proof inflation is heading to target “clearly and at sufficient speed,” and he refused to say what would change his mind. That is a chair reserving the right to tighten.

The flattening tells the real story

Watch what the curve did. Short-term yields jumped while long-term yields slipped — a flattening that carries a specific message. The front end is pricing a Fed that will raise rates; the long end is pricing the slower growth that tighter policy will cause. In plain terms, the bond market believes Warsh will hike, and believes it will hurt. That is a classic late-cycle signal, and it is flashing at the same moment growth has already cooled to 1.5% and the consumer has been retrenching for weeks.

A central bank that tightens into a slowdown is making a bet: that inflation is the greater danger. Warsh just told us which risk keeps him up at night.The Sunday Macro Note

The complications he can't control

Two forces sit outside the Fed's reach and both point the wrong way. The first is the long end, still pinned near multi-decade highs by $40 trillion of federal debt and a wall of AI-linked corporate issuance — a supply problem no rate decision fixes. The second is oil. Middle East tensions are simmering again, and any fresh disruption to energy would re-ignite exactly the inflation Warsh says he is still fighting. He can raise the policy rate. He cannot legislate the price of crude or the appetite for thirty-year paper.

What we're watching

Friday's August jobs report is now the last major data before the Fed meets. A strong number cements the hike Warsh has teed up; a weak one forces an awkward choice between his hawkish signal and a labor market rolling over. Either way, the debate has moved — from when the Fed will cut to whether it is about to hike.
