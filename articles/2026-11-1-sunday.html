The Economy Runs Hot. The AI Build-Out Runs on Credit.

Third-quarter growth hit 3.4% while PCE inflation rose to 3.6% and real incomes fell. Three of the four biggest AI spenders now need outside money, the Fed is leaning toward December, and Tuesday’s midterms add a political variable to both.

AM
Arshia Morshedian
Macro Strategist
SUN · NOV 1 · 2026

This week’s data described an economy most central bankers would envy and most households would not. Output grew at a 3.4% annual rate in the third quarter. PCE inflation rose to 3.6% in September, the fastest since May. Real disposable income fell and the saving rate dropped to its lowest since April. At the same time, the four largest AI spenders told investors that the build-out now costs more than their businesses generate, and markets punished three of them. The Fed held on Wednesday with two votes to hike. The 10-year yield fell to 4.93%, down from more than 5.30% earlier in October, as Treasurys finally behaved like a haven. And on Tuesday the country votes in the midterms. Each of these threads runs into the same question: who funds the next year of growth, and at what price?

1. Growth is strong. Households are not.

The first estimate of third-quarter GDP beat expectations. Consumer spending grew at a 3.8% pace, the same as the spring, and business investment in computers and other equipment was strong. Strategic Petroleum Reserve sales fell, which lifted federal spending. On the surface this is an economy that does not need help from the Fed; Chair Kevin Warsh had said on Wednesday that growth likely topped 3%.

The monthly figures underneath are less comfortable. PCE prices rose 0.5% in September alone. Inflation-adjusted disposable income fell 0.3%, and the saving rate dropped to 3.8%. Households are keeping spending up by saving less, not by earning more. The quarterly PCE price index slowed to a 2.1% annual rate from 5.0% in the spring, when the oil shock first hit, but September’s monthly reading says the relief is not lasting. With mortgage rates having touched a three-year high of 7.4% earlier in October and Brent still above $110, the squeeze on real incomes is the slow-moving risk in a fast-moving economy.

2. The AI build-out became a funding story

A month ago the debate was whether AI demand was real. This month every large spender proved that it is, and three of the four showed that paying for it now requires outside money. Meta spent $34.91 billion on capital projects in the third quarter against $32.79 billion of operating cash flow, then said it plans to raise up to $95 billion in equity. Alphabet bought more than $51 billion of property and equipment in the quarter, burned cash, and said spending will rise significantly in 2027. Amazon’s free cash flow over the past 12 months was an outflow of $42.4 billion, compared with an inflow of $14.8 billion a year earlier. Microsoft was the exception: its capital spending came in below plan and free cash flow, though down 27%, stayed comfortably positive at $18.63 billion.

When the largest companies in the world fund capital spending with new equity and debt, they compete with the Treasury for the same pool of savings, and they do it while real yields sit near multi-decade highs. That is why Warsh’s comment on Wednesday mattered more than the decision. He said he believes higher rates can cool borrowing for data centers. In other words, the Fed sees the AI build-out as part of the demand it is trying to slow, not as a productivity story it should look through. The market took the hint: the Nasdaq lost 5.3% in October and the semiconductor index more than 15%, while the Dow gained.

For two years the market paid for AI capacity as if cash were free. This month it learned the price, and the Fed told it the price could rise.Arshia Morshedian

3. The Fed is leaning toward December

The vote to hold was 10 to 2. Governor Lisa Cook, in her first dissent, and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack wanted a quarter-point hike now. The statement said inflation remains elevated and that the pause gives the committee time to gather information on the way back to 2%. Futures put the odds of a December hike near 85% after the meeting. KPMG, writing after Thursday’s PCE report, said it expects at least two more hikes by spring 2027. The Fed also said it will hold fewer policy meetings starting in 2027.

Three things could still talk the Fed out of December. A weak October jobs report on Friday would test its resolve, since September’s payroll gain was only 29,000. A soft October CPI on Nov. 10 would weaken the inflation case. And a deeper AI selloff would tighten financial conditions on its own. None of those is the base case, but all three are live, and two of them arrive in the next ten days.

4. Bonds are acting like a haven again

For most of September and October, stocks and bonds fell together as the 10-year yield climbed above 5.30%, its highest since 2002. This week that relationship broke. On Thursday and Friday, as Alphabet and then Intel fell, Treasurys rallied and the 10-year closed the week at 4.93%. Yields falling while the Fed leans toward a hike says investors are paying for insurance against a growth scare, not betting on easier policy. For a portfolio, it means duration is working as a hedge again for the first time in months. For housing, it is modest relief: Oxford Economics estimates higher rates have priced roughly 5 million more Americans out of buying a home since January.

5. Oil: talks, tankers and a deadline

Brent settled at $113.43 on Friday, near the top of its range since the war with Iran began eight months ago, and the Strait of Hormuz remains closed. A Qatari LNG tanker was struck by unidentified objects in the Gulf on Friday. Iranian negotiators have spent several days with Qatari mediators without a breakthrough. President Trump has said repeatedly there will be no new strike before Tuesday’s vote. Last month the New York Times reported that the U.S. and Israel had drawn up plans for renewed strikes after the midterms. The calendar matters: whatever restraint the election imposed ends on Wednesday.

6. Beyond the U.S.

The European Central Bank held rates on Thursday, but the bigger news was Christine Lagarde’s announcement that she will step down in February, nine months early, leaving the ECB to choose a new president in the middle of an energy shock and after French 10-year yields topped 5% in October. The Bank of Canada raised its rate to 2.5%, and the Bank of Japan held. In Asia, AI worries hit hardest where AI hardware is concentrated: Seoul’s Kospi fell 3.5% on Friday even as Samsung and SK Hynix report booming memory profits. The global pattern is the same as at home. Central banks outside Japan are leaning tighter, energy is the reason, and the markets most exposed to AI capital spending are the most fragile.

7. Tuesday’s vote

Republicans enter the midterms holding the Senate 53 to 47 and a narrow House majority. For markets, the channels that matter are fiscal (12 annual spending bills and the deficit path), trade, oversight of the administration, and, this year, energy and data centers. The cost of living and AI have become cross-party campaign issues, and several state races and local referendums put data center construction directly on the ballot. The historical pattern is friendly: the S&P 500 has posted a positive return in the six months after every midterm since 1970. But averages do not price a specific outcome, and this year the outcome intersects with the two biggest macro variables in the market, oil and AI spending.

What it means for a portfolio

Three adjustments follow from the week. First, distinguish within AI between companies that fund capacity from operating cash and companies that need outside capital; the market is already doing so, and it rewarded Microsoft while punishing Alphabet on the same night. Second, the small-cap and value parts of the market, which gained in October, are being supported by a strong nominal economy and are less exposed to the AI funding question. Third, Treasurys have regained their role as a hedge, which matters with a December hike, a jobs report and an election all inside six weeks. None of this changes the long-term case for AI. It changes what investors will pay for it while money costs 4% at the front end and nearly 5% at ten years.

The macro read

The economy grew 3.4% in the third quarter, but PCE inflation rose to 3.6% and real incomes fell, leaving households spending out of savings. The biggest AI spenders now need outside money to fund the build-out, the Fed held with two votes to hike and signaled that rates can cool data-center borrowing, and December hike odds sit near 85%. The 10-year fell to 4.93% as Treasurys acted as a haven, while Brent held above $113 with Hormuz still closed. Tuesday’s midterms, Friday’s jobs report and the Nov. 10 CPI decide whether December is settled.
