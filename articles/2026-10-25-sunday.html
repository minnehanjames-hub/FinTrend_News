Sunday Macro Note

Oil Is Rising. AI Is Rationing. The Fed Can Fix Neither.

Meta’s first cash outflow in more than a decade turned the AI build-out into a funding question, and Houthi attacks in Saudi Arabia pushed Brent 6% higher to a four-year high. The Fed meets Tuesday and Wednesday with a hold priced and a December hike seen as likely. Neither of the week’s two shocks responds to interest rates.

AM
Arshia Morshedian
Macro Strategist
SUN · OCT 25 · 2026

Last Sunday I argued that markets were tightening financial conditions on the Fed’s behalf, through AI credit, in ways its usual gauges barely register. This week the tightening moved from credit spreads into corporate cash flows, and a second shock landed on top of it. Meta reported its first cash outflow in more than a decade and created a class of non-voting shares it says could be used to raise capital; chip stocks fell 6.7% in a day. Then Houthi attacks in Saudi Arabia pushed Brent to its highest close since mid-2022, about $116.72, up 6.2% on the week.

The Fed meets on Tuesday and Wednesday with its target range at 3.75% to 4.00% after September’s hike. A hold is the consensus: in a Reuters poll, 94 of 100 economists expect one, and all 100 expect a hike in December. Prediction markets put the December odds at roughly 76% to 79%. The hold is the easy part. The harder part for Chair Kevin Warsh is that both of the week’s shocks sit outside the reach of interest rates, and one of them pushes inflation up.

1. The AI build-out became a cash-flow story

For two years the AI spenders paid for data centers out of profits. Meta’s third quarter shows that model reaching its limit. Capital spending of $34.91 billion exceeded the $32.79 billion its operations generated, leaving free cash flow at negative $2.12 billion, against positive $784 million in the second quarter. Over nine months, free cash flow has fallen to about $11 billion from $29.5 billion a year earlier. The new Class C shares, which take effect on Jan. 19, 2027, give Meta a currency to raise money without diluting Mark Zuckerberg’s control. SpaceX, which rose on its results, spent about $19.6 billion on capital projects in the quarter, more than its revenue. Tesla cut its 2026 capex but flagged heavier spending in 2027 and 2028.

The macro point is about who funds the next round. When spending outruns internal cash, the money has to come from bond and equity markets, and it competes for the same pool of savings as the U.S. Treasury, a French government planning record borrowing and every other issuer. That raises the cost of capital for the whole AI supply chain, which is why the chip index fell 6.7% on Thursday and CoreWeave about 10%, even though AI revenue keeps beating forecasts. Microsoft has guided to $255 billion to $260 billion of capital spending in its new fiscal year, and Alphabet to $195 billion to $205 billion this year. Both report on Wednesday, hours after the Fed.

2. The oil shock is spreading to Saudi Arabia

For most of this year the energy shock was about the Strait of Hormuz. Increasingly it is also about Saudi Arabia. The Houthis were already firing at Saudi targets in September, including the Red Sea export port of Yanbu. This week Iran-backed Houthi attacks hit an airport in Dammam and an Aramco oil field, and said they attacked the kingdom’s airport and oil facilities again on Saturday. On Thursday the New York Times reported that the U.S. and Israel had drawn up plans for renewed strikes on Iran after the Nov. 3 midterms, and on Saturday U.S. diplomatic missions across the region reportedly tightened security after intelligence warnings about Iranian plots. Brent has now traded between $97 and about $117 this month.

The pass-through is already visible. In S&P Global’s flash PMI, selling prices rose at the fastest rate in four years as oil fed into goods and services, and the survey’s chief economist said inflation could move further above the Fed’s 2% target. Consumers see it too: the University of Michigan’s year-ahead inflation expectation is 4.7%. A supply shock like this lowers growth and raises prices at the same time. Higher rates can slow demand, but they do not reopen a pipeline or protect an oil field.

3. The bond market is pricing real rates, not panic

The 10-year yield fell below 5% on Thursday as money left the AI trade and ended the week at 4.99%, down from 5.09%. But look at what is inside that yield. Bloomberg noted this week that inflation breakevens sit slightly below their pre-war levels, which means this year’s rise in yields has come almost entirely from real rates. Thursday’s five-year TIPS auction cleared at a real yield of 2.367%, and last month’s 10-year TIPS reopening priced at 2.653%, the highest in nearly 18 years. The market believes the Fed will keep long-run inflation in check. It is charging a high real price for that belief.

High real rates are what make the AI funding question bite. A company borrowing to build data centers pays a real return before it earns one, and the hurdle is now well above where it was when most of these plans were drawn up. The same forces are visible abroad. France’s 10-year yield rose above 5% this week for the first time since 2002, and its premium over German Bunds reached the widest since the euro-area debt crisis, after riots over living costs and as a minority government tries to pass €54 billion of savings. The Treasury’s 20-year auction tailed on Wednesday. Long-term borrowing is getting more expensive everywhere, not just for AI.

4. What the Fed does on Wednesday

There are no new projections at this meeting, so the statement and Warsh’s press conference are all the market gets, and Warsh dislikes forward guidance. PIMCO expects a hold with a clear tightening bias. The more useful signal may be the dissents: Beth Hammack, Lorie Logan and Neel Kashkari voted for a hike in July, and any repeat would tell markets December is closer to settled.

The case for staying patient is real. Long-run inflation expectations held at 3.5% in the final Michigan reading rather than rising, breakevens are contained, and the market is already tightening through AI credit and a 5% long bond. The case for leaning hawkish is just as strong: the flash composite PMI is at 57.6, with S&P Global saying it is consistent with growth near a 5% annualized rate; the Atlanta Fed’s GDPNow model put third-quarter growth at 3.5% as of Oct. 20; jobless claims fell to 194,000; and oil rose 6% in a week. My read is that Warsh holds, keeps December firmly open and offers no comfort on the long end. Thursday’s first estimate of third-quarter GDP and September’s PCE inflation, expected near 3.7%, will test whichever message he chooses. The ECB, which raised its deposit rate to 2.50% in September, decides the same day.

5. Beyond the U.S.

The pressure on Europe is fiscal as much as monetary. France plans to borrow a record €340 billion next year and its debt reached €3.6 trillion, about 119% of GDP, in the second quarter. With energy prices high and growth fragile, the ECB faces the same bind as the Fed on Thursday, with less room. In Asia, Seoul’s Kospi, dominated by Samsung and SK Hynix, fell 3.7% on Tuesday and rose 3.3% on Wednesday, a measure of how much of global equity risk now runs through the AI chip trade. Gold slipped briefly below $4,000 on Friday, another sign that high real yields weigh even on havens.

What it means for a portfolio

This week drew a sharper line through the AI trade. The market punished spending it could not see being paid for and rewarded companies whose cash covers their plans, and it did so while small caps rose 2% and defensive stocks held up. That argues for judging AI exposure by free cash flow and balance sheets rather than by revenue growth alone. Energy remains the natural offset while Brent sits above $110 and the Saudi attacks continue. In bonds, Thursday showed that Treasurys still work as a hedge when the AI trade wobbles, and real yields near multi-decade highs offer more cushion than they have in years, but an oil shock limits how far yields can fall. This week, watch three things: how many Fed officials dissent for a hike, whether Microsoft and Alphabet’s operating cash flow still covers their spending, and Thursday’s PCE print.

The macro read

Two shocks hit at once. Meta’s first cash outflow in over a decade showed the AI build-out moving from being funded by profits to being funded by markets, just as real yields sit near multi-decade highs. And Houthi attacks in Saudi Arabia pushed Brent 6.2% higher to about $116.72, feeding selling prices that are rising at the fastest pace in four years. The Fed is expected to hold on Wednesday with a December hike seen as likely, but neither shock responds to rates. Watch the dissents, Microsoft and Alphabet’s cash flow, and Thursday’s PCE.
