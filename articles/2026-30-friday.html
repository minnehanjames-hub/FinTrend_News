Friday Recap · Week of Oct 26

The Rebound Ran Into Big Tech’s Cash Bill.

Three straight gains put the S&P 500 within 1% of its record before Alphabet’s worst day on record and Intel’s worst since 1974 sent the Nasdaq down 2.9% on Friday. The S&P lost 0.6% on the week; the Nasdaq lost 2.0% and the Russell 2000 gained 1.3%.

HWL
Hunter William Lang
Markets Editor
FRI · OCT 30 · 2026

For three days this week the AI trade looked repaired. Anthropic’s IPO filing, a Fed that held as expected and a fourth straight gain for chip stocks put the S&P 500 within 1% of its record on Wednesday night. Then the bills came due. Alphabet fell 12.6% on Thursday, its worst day since it went public, after a revenue miss and a promise to spend significantly more in 2027. On Friday, Intel fell 28.6%, its worst day since 1974, Amazon disclosed a $42.4 billion free cash outflow over the past year, and the Nasdaq dropped 2.9%. The S&P 500 lost 1.8% on Friday and 0.6% on the week to 7,580.07. The Nasdaq lost 2.0% on the week. The Russell 2000 gained 1.3%.

Thursday: Alphabet’s worst day

Alphabet’s quarter was not a disaster on the surface. Revenue rose 25% to $127.58 billion and Google Cloud more than doubled to $30.7 billion. But revenue came in just under forecasts, search growth slowed, earnings missed badly because of an $8.7 billion loss on equity investments, and the company burned cash after spending more than $51 billion on property and equipment in the quarter. Its finance chief said spending will rise significantly in 2027, with details to come on the next earnings call. The Class A shares fell 12.6%, the steepest one-day drop in their more than 20-year history, and were the main reason the S&P 500 fell 0.3% even though about four of every five stocks in the index rose.

That gap between the index and the average stock is the story of the week. Eli Lilly rose 6.6% and Mastercard 4.2% on earnings. DoorDash rose 5.6% after beating forecasts and agreeing to buy Instacart in a deal valued at $17 billion. Norwegian Cruise Line jumped 13.4%. The Russell 2000 gained 1.0% on Thursday and 3.0% for the week through that day.

Friday: Amazon and Intel finish the job

Amazon’s numbers were strong almost everywhere except cash. Sales rose 12% to $201.9 billion, Amazon Web Services grew 39% to $46.0 billion, and operating margin reached a record 13.9%. Earnings per share fell to $1.89 from $1.95, but only because a year ago it booked $9.5 billion of gains on its Anthropic stake. The problem was free cash flow: an outflow of $42.4 billion over the past 12 months, against an inflow of $14.8 billion a year earlier, as spending on property and equipment jumped. Amazon guided fourth-quarter sales to $240 billion to $247 billion. The stock pared an early loss and closed down 1.9%.

Intel did more damage. Its quarterly profit beat estimates, but its forecast for the final quarter of the year fell short, and chief executive Lip-Bu Tan said the PC industry continues to feel pressure. The stock fell 28.6%, its worst day since 1974, according to the Associated Press. AMD fell 10.9%, Dell 10.8%, HP 9.6% and Nvidia 4.2%, which made Nvidia the heaviest weight on the S&P 500. The Philadelphia semiconductor index lost 7.2% and finished October down more than 15%. Crypto added a second shock: hackers stole more than $6 billion from the Binance exchange, bitcoin fell below $77,000, and Coinbase lost 13.6% despite better-than-expected results.

The hyperscalers keep proving the demand is real. This week they also proved the cash is not unlimited, and the market cares more about the second fact.Hunter William Lang

Joe Tigay of Equity Armor Investments told the AP the AI trade is moving into a “show me” phase. That is right, with one refinement: the market is not asking for proof of demand any more. It is asking for proof that demand can be funded out of operating cash. Microsoft passed that test this week. Meta, Alphabet and Amazon, in different ways, did not.

The data: a booming economy, squeezed households

Thursday’s first estimate showed the economy grew at a 3.4% annual rate in the third quarter, up from 2.2% in the second, with consumer spending up 3.8% and business investment in computers and equipment strong. But the monthly report on incomes and prices showed the cost. PCE inflation rose 0.5% in September and 3.6% from a year earlier, the fastest pace since May. Core PCE rose 0.3% on the month and 3.1% on the year. Disposable income, adjusted for inflation, fell 0.3%, and the saving rate dropped to 3.8%, the lowest since April. Households are spending more than they earn in real terms, and they are doing it with fuel prices still high. Weekly jobless claims fell again.

Central banks: a hawkish hold and an exit

The Fed held at 3.75% to 4.00% on Wednesday, with Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack voting for a hike, and futures ended the week pricing a December increase as the most likely outcome. The European Central Bank also held on Thursday, but the news was Christine Lagarde’s announcement that she will step down as president in February, nine months before her term ends. The euro dipped and then recovered. The Bank of Japan held too, and on Friday the Nikkei 225 fell 2.1% while Seoul’s Kospi dropped 3.5% on AI worries. Earlier in the week the Bank of Canada raised its rate to 2.5%.

Bonds and oil: the haven bid returns

The 10-year yield ended the week at 4.93%, down from about 5.05% on Wednesday and from more than 5.30% earlier this month. Falling yields during an equity selloff mean Treasurys are working as a hedge again, which was not true for most of September and October. Oil went the other way on Friday: Brent rose 2.4% to $113.43, near the top of its range since the war with Iran began, on a day when a Qatari LNG tanker was struck by unidentified objects in the Gulf. Brent still finished about 2.8% lower for the week. Energy was the best S&P sector on Friday, with Exxon Mobil up 1.8% and Chevron 1.7% on better-than-expected profits.

October in the books

Measured from the Sept. 30 close, the S&P 500 lost about 0.9% in October and the Nasdaq about 5.3%, while the Dow gained about 1.7% and the Russell 2000 about 0.5%. Reuters reported that the S&P technology sector had its worst month since March 2025 and communication services its worst since April 2022. Energy and the parts of the market far from AI did the work. Earnings did not let the market down: of the 299 S&P 500 companies that have reported, 82% beat estimates and LSEG expects 31.6% earnings growth for the quarter. The market’s problem is not profit. It is the price of the spending that is supposed to produce next year’s profit.

The bottom line

The S&P 500 is 3.1% below its record, the Nasdaq 7.8%, and the small caps are near the top of their range. Next week brings Apple’s results on Monday, the midterm elections on Tuesday and the October jobs report on Friday, with Brent above $110 and a December hike priced as likely. The question for the AI leaders is unchanged from last week, only louder: can they fund the build-out without leaning on shareholders and bondholders? The question for everything else is whether a 3.4% economy can keep carrying 3.6% inflation.

The bottom line

The S&P 500 fell 0.6% on the week to 7,580.07 and the Nasdaq 2.0%, after Alphabet’s record 12.6% drop on Thursday and Intel’s 28.6% collapse on Friday reversed a three-day rally that had taken the index within 1% of its record. The Russell 2000 rose 1.3%. The Fed held with two hike dissents, GDP grew 3.4% and PCE inflation hit 3.6%, Lagarde announced her exit, and the 10-year fell to 4.93% as Treasurys acted as a haven. Brent ended at $113.43. Apple, the midterms and the jobs report come next.
