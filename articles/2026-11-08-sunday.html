Sunday Macro Note

Hiring Is Up, Oil Is Down. The AI Build-Out Found Its Ceiling.

October payrolls rose 165,000 and Brent fell about 13%, its biggest weekly drop since 2020. Both ease the Fed’s dilemma; neither helped the AI trade, which now faces political limits on construction on top of its funding strain. Tuesday’s CPI decides how much room the Fed gets.

AM
Arshia Morshedian
Macro Strategist
SUN · NOV 8 · 2026

Two of this week’s three big macro stories were good news. Employers added 165,000 jobs in October and unemployment fell to 4.1%. Brent crude fell roughly 13%, its biggest weekly drop since March 2020, as the midterm result raised the odds that Washington pushes to wind down the war with Iran. In most years that combination would lift stocks. This week the S&P 500 lost 2.7%, its worst week since April 2025, because the third story overwhelmed the other two: the election put a political limit on how fast the AI build-out can grow, just as the market had started to question how it will be paid for. The Fed now has a stronger labor market, cheaper oil and a weaker AI trade to weigh before December.

FedWatch after payrolls

1. The labor market re-accelerated

October’s report was strong in every direction. The 165,000 gain compares with an average of 48,000 a month over the prior year. Revisions added 84,000 jobs to August and September, putting the three-month average near 137,000. Unemployment fell to 4.1%, the bottom of its 4.1% to 4.3% range since March. Hiring was broad, led by professional and business services, health care, transportation and warehousing, and manufacturing. Average hourly earnings rose 0.4% on the month and 3.1% on the year.

Two details matter for the Fed. First, wages are rising 3.1% a year while PCE prices rose 3.6% in September, so real pay is still falling. This is not a wage-price spiral; it is an energy shock eating into paychecks, which is why sentiment had been so weak. Second, the September hike has not cooled hiring yet. A labor market adding jobs at this pace with the funds rate near 4% removes the Fed’s main reason to wait, and futures moved accordingly: the odds of a December hike rose to about 77% after the report.

2. Oil gave back part of the war premium

Brent began the week above $112 and finished just under $98. The single biggest move came on Wednesday, when Vice President JD Vance linked part of the Republican losses to the war and traders priced more pressure for a settlement. Later in the week President Trump discussed de-escalation with Qatar’s emir and Iran was reported to be reviewing mediators’ proposals. The gap is still wide: Tehran wants the U.S. naval blockade lifted, sanctions eased and frozen assets released before it fully reopens the Strait of Hormuz, and attacks on shipping in the Gulf continue. Brent has traded between roughly $70 and $120 since just before the war began, and it remains in the upper half of that range.

Even so, a $15 fall in crude is the most useful disinflationary development since the war started. GasBuddy’s Patrick De Haan said the national average for gasoline could fall below $4 a gallon if prices hold, and the University of Michigan’s preliminary November survey showed year-ahead inflation expectations easing and sentiment improving. The October CPI on Tuesday will not capture much of this, since crude was more expensive through October than it is now. November’s report, due Dec. 10, the day after the Fed decides, is the one that will.

Cheaper oil helps the Fed. Strong hiring takes the help away. The AI selloff tightens conditions on its own. December is now a three-way argument.Arshia Morshedian

3. The AI build-out met the ballot box

For two weeks the market judged the AI leaders on cash: Meta, Alphabet and Amazon showed that the build-out now needs outside funding. This week it judged them on permission. Democrat Gina Hinojosa, who ran against data center projects, unseated Texas Gov. Greg Abbott in a state that holds about 27% of planned U.S. data center capacity, according to Wells Fargo estimates cited by CNBC. Data Center Watch counted 32 of 34 called governor races going to candidates who favor tighter rules, and restrictions passed in 44 of 45 completed local referendums. UBS estimated that construction pauses could put about 30% of expected 2028 capacity additions at risk.

The macro channel is investment. Spending on computers, equipment and data centers has been one of the largest drivers of U.S. growth this year, and the third-quarter GDP report again credited equipment investment. If permitting slows, the hit shows up in 2027 and 2028 growth, in electricity demand forecasts and in the order books of chip and power-equipment makers. The financing channel tightened too: five-year credit default swap spreads on CoreWeave reached a record, and smaller cloud operators that borrow to build were hit hardest on Thursday. Even private markets are adjusting. Bloomberg reported on Friday that prospective buyers of Anthropic’s IPO were discussing a valuation of about $1.2 trillion to $1.3 trillion, below the roughly $1.4 trillion to $1.5 trillion talked about earlier.

Chair Kevin Warsh said last week that higher rates can cool borrowing for data centers. Politics is now doing part of that work for him, and with less precision. That matters for the December decision, because a slower AI build-out cools both demand and some of the price pressure in power and memory chips, even if it does nothing for oil.

4. Bonds and the Fed

The 10-year yield ended the week at 4.86%, below the 4.93% of a week earlier and well below the more than 5.30% it reached in October. Three forces pulled it down: lower oil, a flight to safety on Thursday, and a divided government that makes large new deficit-financed programs less likely. The strong jobs report pushed back only modestly. The bond market is telling the Fed that it can hike in December without long rates rising sharply, which makes a hike easier to deliver.

The case against December now rests on inflation. If Tuesday’s CPI shows core prices rising about 0.2% on the month, as some forecasters expect, and oil stays below $100 into December, the committee could argue that the September hike and the fall in energy prices are doing the job. If core runs at 0.4% or more, a hike becomes close to certain. Producer prices follow on Friday.

5. Beyond the U.S.

The Bank of England raised its rate to 4.00% on Thursday, its first increase since 2023. Catherine Mann voted for a half-point move. Governor Andrew Bailey said the hike should not be read as the start of a long sequence, but the Bank expects inflation, 3.5% in September, to peak at an average of 4.8% in the second quarter of 2027. Australia’s central bank raised rates for the fifth time this year. The global tightening cycle is an energy story, and the drop in oil this week is the first real relief it has had.

The AI selloff hit hardest where AI hardware is concentrated. Seoul’s Kospi fell 5.3% on Thursday and Tokyo and Seoul each fell about 6% on Friday, while Germany’s DAX lost a more modest 1.9%. Europe’s smaller exposure to AI stocks cushioned it, and its larger exposure to energy prices means it gains more from cheaper oil.

What it means for a portfolio

The macro backdrop improved this week and the AI backdrop deteriorated, and portfolios should reflect both. A strong labor market and falling oil support the cyclical, domestic and small-cap parts of the market, and consumer companies that benefit from cheaper fuel. Treasurys worked as a hedge on Thursday and should keep doing so while the AI trade reprices. Within AI, the distinction now has three parts: companies that fund capacity from operating cash, companies that depend on outside capital, and companies that depend on new construction in places where voters have just asked for limits. The first group has the most room. The last has the least. Tuesday’s CPI decides how much patience the Fed can afford to show any of them.

The macro read

October payrolls rose 165,000, unemployment fell to 4.1% and wages rose 3.1%, lifting December hike odds to about 77%. Brent fell roughly 13%, its biggest weekly drop since 2020, on expectations of pressure to end the war with Iran. But the S&P 500 lost 2.7% as the midterms put political limits on data center construction on top of the AI build-out’s funding strain. The 10-year fell to 4.86%. The Bank of England hiked to 4.00%. Tuesday’s CPI and Friday’s PPI decide whether cheaper oil gives the Fed room to wait in December.
