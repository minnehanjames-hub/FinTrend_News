Sunday Macro Note

The Market Is Tightening for the Fed. The Fed Says It Can’t Feel It.

Wholesale prices rose 6.3% from a year earlier, the most since 2022, yet the 10-year fell to 5.09%. The AI build-out has turned into a credit story — chip stocks lost 11% in a week and the cost of insuring CoreWeave’s debt hit a record — and that is tightening conditions in a corner the Fed’s gauges barely register. With Brent near $110, December is still the base case.

AM
Arshia Morshedian
Macro Strategist
SUN · OCT 18 · 2026

Last Sunday this note argued that inflation expectations, not payrolls, would decide the Fed’s next move, and that Wednesday’s core CPI would show whether the energy shock was spreading. It is spreading. Core consumer prices rose 0.3% in September against 0.2% expected, and the headline rate climbed to 3.7%. On Thursday producer prices rose 1.3% in a single month, the biggest jump since March 2022, for an annual pace of 6.3%. Goods prices at the wholesale level rose 2.6%, the largest monthly increase since the series began in 2009.

On the old playbook, that mix sends long-term yields to new highs. Instead the 10-year Treasury yield fell about 15 basis points on the week to 5.09%, near its lowest close since late September. The bond market found something it fears more than the Fed: the cost of financing the AI build-out. Chip stocks had their worst week since April 2025, the price of insuring CoreWeave’s debt hit a record, and money ran to Treasuries. That flight is a form of tightening. The question for the next ten days is whether the Fed counts it.

1. The energy shock is in the pipeline

The details of the September consumer price report were worse than the headline. Gasoline jumped 9.1% on the month and energy prices are up 20.4% from a year ago, which was expected. What was not expected was the core: shelter, airfares and used cars pushed prices excluding food and energy up 0.3%, for a 2.6% annual rate. Real average hourly earnings fell 0.5% on the month and are down 0.7% from a year earlier. Households are paying more and earning less in real terms.

The producer price report explains why the core may not be finished. Services prices at the wholesale level rose 0.8%, and the core measure that strips out food, energy and trade margins rose 0.5% on the month and 5.0% from a year ago. Import prices rose 1.1% in September and 8.1% over the year. These are the costs businesses pay each other before anything reaches a shelf, and they tend to show up in consumer prices with a lag of a few months. Add retail sales up 0.8% against 0.3% expected, jobless claims at 199,000 and industrial production up 0.7%, and on paper this is an economy running hot, not one that needs relief.

The Fed heard it. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack said on Thursday that if an inflationary mindset takes hold, the Fed may need to move by more than it otherwise would. Chair Kevin Warsh, speaking in Bangkok on Friday, declined to say what the committee will do on Oct. 28 and described central banks around the world raising rates together, with, as he put it, no free riders. Futures now price only about a one-in-six chance of an October hike, but CME data put the odds of a December move at 74%, up from 50% a month ago.

2. The AI build-out became a credit story

The reason yields fell anyway is that the AI trade changed character this week. For most of this year it was an earnings story: companies spending on chips and data centers, and suppliers booking the revenue. It is now also a financing story. Bank of America estimates that the five largest spenders — Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle — will spend about $807 billion on AI this year, and that their combined free cash flow will swing from $191 billion in 2025 to minus $63 billion in 2026. When the richest companies in the world stop generating spare cash, the rest of the build-out has to be paid for with debt.

That debt is where the stress showed up. CoreWeave, which rents out GPU capacity and has become the market’s gauge of AI credit risk, saw its five-year credit default swaps hit a record of about 1,195 basis points on Thursday, a level that by standard calculations implies a better-than-60% chance of default within five years. Its 9.75% notes due 2031 traded near 82 cents on the dollar, yielding about 15%, up from roughly 12% two months ago. In the second quarter it generated $679 million of operating cash flow against about $6.4 billion of equipment spending, and its net interest bill more than doubled to $640 million. CoreWeave and Nebius each lost about a quarter of their value over the week. Allianz’s chief investment officer told the Financial Times that data-center bonds are having their “Darwin moment.”

The equity market priced the same thing. The Philadelphia semiconductor index fell 11.4% on the week, its worst since April 2025. Memory makers took the brunt after reports of progress at China’s CXMT: Micron lost 14.6%, Samsung Electronics 17.8% and SK Hynix 24.3%. South Korea’s Kospi fell about 8% on Thursday alone. The Nasdaq ended the week down 4.5%, its worst since June, while the Dow, with far less AI exposure, rose 0.7%.

3. Tightening the Fed can’t feel

Here is the problem. On Thursday — the same day the Kospi fell 8% and CoreWeave’s default insurance hit a record — Hammack also said she was “not feeling that there’s restraint coming from financial conditions.” By the usual measures, she is right. Those indices lean heavily on Treasury yields, investment-grade spreads, the dollar and the broad stock market. The 10-year fell, the dollar slipped, the S&P 500 is only about 2% below its Oct. 6 record and the Dow rose on the week. On that dashboard, conditions eased.

But the tightening is concentrated in exactly the part of the economy the Fed said it was worried about. September’s minutes named AI-related investment as one of three sources of inflation pressure, alongside energy and tariffs. If financing costs are now high enough to choke off the marginal data center — the one built by a company borrowing at 15% rather than a megacap paying cash — then one source of demand-driven inflation is already being squeezed without a rate hike. Add real wages down 0.7% from a year earlier and consumer sentiment at 46.3, and the economy is absorbing two squeezes at once.

That is the case against hiking in October, and the market agrees with it. The case for December is just as clear. The energy shock is not a demand problem that tighter credit fixes, wholesale core inflation at 5.0% says more pass-through is coming, and long-run inflation expectations at 3.5% are the number the Fed has said matters most. Bank of America’s Michael Hartnett goes further, arguing that an early hike could restore the Fed’s credibility and pull long-term yields lower. The likeliest outcome is the one priced: a hold on Oct. 28 with hawkish language, and a hike in December unless AI credit stress spreads beyond the weakest borrowers.

4. Energy: the $110 problem

Oil gave the Fed no relief. Brent touched $110 on Friday for the first time since May and settled at $109.86, up 3.7% on the day and about 5% on the week; it was near $72 in late February, before the war began. The Strait of Hormuz is open in name and contested in practice. UK Maritime Trade Operations counts at least 23 vessels attacked near the strait since Oct. 1, making this the busiest month for attacks since March. On Saturday Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said a supertanker carrying more than two million barrels caught fire after hitting a mine; the U.S. military says the waterway has been cleared of mines and that ships are being struck by projectiles. Kpler data show flows through the strait at 11.6 million barrels a day in the week to Oct. 13, about 68% of the pre-war 17.1 million. And on Sunday Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, said Tehran’s conditions for reopening it, including an end to the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports, still stand.

Washington is reaching for the levers it controls. On Friday President Trump signed an order putting on hold the Graham Act sanctions on Russia, which he signed into law in September, saying the pause is in the national interest; it follows a deal announced a week earlier for the U.S. to buy Russian diesel. Both moves aim at pump prices — the national average is $4.33 a gallon, against $3.06 a year ago — before the Nov. 3 midterms. They may shave the margin. They cannot replace the third of Hormuz’s pre-war flow that is still missing. For inflation expectations, the price that matters is the one households see every week, and it is not coming down by decree.

5. The world is tightening in sync

Warsh’s description of a synchronized global tightening shows up first in the weakest markets. On Friday MSCI said Indonesia no longer qualifies as an emerging market and put it on a path to standalone status, a step below the frontier downgrade it had warned of. Jakarta stocks fell as much as 11.4%, their steepest one-day drop since January 1998, leaving the market down nearly 40% this year, and the rupiah weakened to 18,070 per dollar, close to its record low. When a U.S. 10-year pays 5%, capital becomes selective about everything else, and markets with transparency questions are the first to be cut. China reports third-quarter GDP on Monday, the next read on whether the world’s second-largest economy is adding to global demand or exporting its spare capacity.

What it means for a portfolio

Read last week’s bond rally for what it is: a safety bid, not a sign the Fed is done. With a December hike about 74% priced, the short end has little room to rally. Longer bonds are now behaving as a hedge against the AI trade rather than a casualty of inflation — useful, but a hedge that reverses quickly if AI credit stabilizes and the inflation data take over again. Keep duration modest. In equities, separate AI companies that fund their spending from their own cash flow from those that borrow to do it; until financing costs ease, free cash flow matters more than revenue growth. Energy and the defensive sectors that led on Friday remain the natural offsets while Brent sits above $100. This week, listen to Meta on Wednesday for how next year’s spending will be paid for, and watch Friday’s final Michigan survey: if long-run expectations hold at 3.5% or rise, December stays the base case.

The macro read

September’s data confirmed the energy shock is spreading: core CPI rose 0.3%, producer prices 6.3% from a year earlier and wholesale core 5.0%. Yet the 10-year fell to 5.09% as the AI build-out became a credit story — big-tech free cash flow is forecast to turn negative, CoreWeave’s default insurance hit a record and chip stocks fell 11.4%. That is real tightening the Fed’s usual gauges barely register. Brent at $109.86 and long-run expectations at 3.5% keep a December hike about 74% priced. Watch Meta’s spending plans and Friday’s Michigan survey.
