Financial Repression, Quietly

The Treasury doubled its bond buybacks and called it a liquidity measure. Deutsche Bank called it something else. When a government starts buying its own long bonds to hold down yields it can't otherwise control, the label matters less than the precedent.

Arshia MorshedianMacro — The Sunday Macro Note
Sun · Aug 23, 2026

The official language was procedural. The Treasury would “provide greater liquidity support in longer-dated nominal sectors” — bureaucratic phrasing for a decision to at least double the size of its bond buybacks and step in as a larger buyer of the very debt the market has been refusing. It arrived the same week U.S. public debt crossed $40 trillion, with the 30-year yield at a 19-year high. Strip away the phrasing and the picture is stark: Washington is now intervening to hold down the cost of its own borrowing.

The buyers' strike at the long end

The long end of the Treasury curve has been under a slow-motion assault since June. The sell-off is not a growth story — growth is fine — it is a supply story. A federal deficit that keeps widening, a debt load that just passed $40 trillion four and a half years after crossing $30 trillion, and a market increasingly unwilling to absorb thirty-year paper at these yields. The Treasury's own 20-year auction last week cleared at the second-highest yield since the bond was reintroduced in 2020. That is what a buyers' strike looks like in the data.

Why they call it repression

Deutsche Bank's read was blunt: the buyback expansion, together with last month's coordinated intervention to prop up the yen, are “signs of increasing administration unease” and look like a “soft-form” financial repression — the quiet use of a government's balance sheet to hold borrowing costs below where a free market would set them. The tell is that it barely worked. The buyback is tiny next to a $30-trillion-plus market; yields fell for a single session and then climbed straight back. The dollars don't move the curve. The symbolism does — and symbolism has a short shelf life.

A central bank sets the price of money for a year. When a Treasury starts setting the price of thirty, you are no longer in an ordinary market.The Sunday Macro Note

The structural competitor nobody voted for

There is a second bidder for long-duration money that makes Washington's job harder, and it isn't a foreign central bank. It is corporate America's AI build-out. Hyperscalers have issued more than $200 billion of investment-grade debt this year, and AI-linked financing now makes up close to 40% of long-dated high-grade supply. Every dollar of thirty-year corporate paper sold to fund a data center is a dollar of duration demand not available to the Treasury. The government and its largest companies are now competing for the same scarce pool of long-term savings — and that competition is a permanent feature, not a passing squall.

What we're watching

Friday's Jackson Hole keynote from Kevin Warsh. A Fed leaning hawkish would validate the long end's fears and undercut the Treasury's attempt to cap yields — fiscal and monetary policy pulling in opposite directions in full view. Watch the 30-year into and out of the speech: it, not the S&P, is where this story is being written.
