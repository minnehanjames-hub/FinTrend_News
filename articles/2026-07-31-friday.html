The Trade That Wouldn't Break

The week built to break the AI trade instead reaffirmed it. A hawkish Fed and 2007-high yields met Microsoft's blowout and Amazon's AWS reacceleration — and the market chose, once more, to pay for the buildout it had punished a week earlier.

Hunter William Lang · Markets Correspondent
Friday, July 31, 2026

This was the week built to break the AI trade. Instead it reaffirmed it. Four of the five biggest companies in America reported into a hawkish Fed, a bond market at multi-year highs, and a chip sector already in a bear market — and by Friday, the trade that had been in freefall for a month came roaring back. Microsoft and Amazon delivered exactly what investors had stopped believing they would see, and the market decided, once more, to pay for the AI buildout it had punished a week earlier.

The capex verdict flips

Rewind one week. Alphabet grew Google Cloud 82% and fell 7% on a spending raise; Tesla and Intel beat and fell. The tape's message looked like a blanket rejection of AI capital expenditure. This week rewrote it. Microsoft's Azure accelerated to 43% and it guided to 45%, with capital spending actually coming in below fears — the stock rose 16% Thursday, its best day in years. Amazon then reported AWS growth of 37% to $42.2 billion, its fastest in eighteen quarters, and jumped 13% Friday. The four hyperscalers now guide to a combined $720–745 billion of 2026 capital spending, and this week the market chose to fund it.

The difference from a week ago wasn't the size of the checks. It was that Microsoft and Amazon paired the spending with cloud acceleration, while the names that got punished paired it with decelerating growth or collapsing margins. Meta was this week's cautionary case — revenue up 28%, but the operating margin down to 31% and the capex floor raised, and the stock fell 9%. The market is no longer scared of AI spending. It is scared of AI spending that doesn't show up in the top line.

The week's real lesson

Apple, the other exception

Apple was the week's odd stock out for the opposite reason. It has no hyperscale capex bill to defend, and it posted its strongest June quarter ever — revenue of about $109.4 billion, up 16%, with record Services. But Services growth and Greater China both came in light, a chunk of the margin beat came from one-time tariff refunds, and the forward outlook disappointed. After a 25% year-to-date run that had briefly made it the first $5 trillion company on Tuesday, that was enough to send the stock down about 7% Friday. Expectations, not execution, were the problem.

The cross-current: good data, hawkish Fed

Underneath the earnings drama, the macro data actually cooperated. Core PCE — the Fed's preferred gauge — cooled to 3.3% year over year with a soft 0.1% monthly print, and headline PCE fell to 3.7%, helped by a 5.9% drop in energy prices during June's brief Middle East lull. Second-quarter GDP slowed to 1.5%. On its own, that is a disinflationary, slowing-growth picture the Fed would normally welcome.

The catch is what the bond market is doing with it. The 10-year yield hit 4.737% on Friday, its highest since January 2025, and the 30-year sat near 5.22%, levels last seen in 2007. With crude up more than 20% in July and a Fed that just signaled it won't ease, the long end is pricing inflation risk the backward-looking data doesn't yet show. That is the tension into August: cooling prints, a hawkish central bank, and a bond market that believes the hawks.

Add it up and the AI trade passed its hardest test of the year. The leadership that had been bleeding out — mega-cap software, the hyperscalers, the chip complex — came back to life, with the Kospi's roughly 18% surge and SK Hynix's limit-up move signaling the memory and semiconductor trade found a floor. Breadth was healthy: the Russell 2000 outperformed on Friday, and the rebound was broad rather than narrow. What it did not resolve is the thing sitting above all of it — a Fed that wants inflation lower and a bond market that isn't convinced it will get there. Jackson Hole, August 27–29, is the next word.

What changed this week

  • The AI trade survived. Microsoft and Amazon reaccelerated cloud growth and were rewarded; the month-long derating in tech and chips reversed hard into month-end.
  • Capex is fine if growth follows. The market funded $720–745B of hyperscaler spending this week — but punished Meta's margin collapse and shrugged at Apple's light guidance.
  • The data cooled; the Fed didn't. Core PCE eased to 3.3% and GDP slowed to 1.5%, yet the 10-year hit its highest since January 2025 on a hawkish hold and 20%+ July oil.
  • Jackson Hole is the catalyst. With no forward guidance, Warsh's late-August remarks are the market's next real read on whether September brings a hike.
