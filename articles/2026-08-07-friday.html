The Week the Labor Market Blinked

Employers shed 23,000 jobs in July — the sort of number that usually rattles a market at record highs. Instead the S&P closed at a fresh peak, betting that a cooling jobs market buys the Fed room to hold off on the rate hikes Kevin Warsh has been signaling.

Hunter William LangMarkets — The Friday Recap
Fri · Aug 7, 2026

For six months the fear on Wall Street has been that the Federal Reserve, led by an inflation-wary Kevin Warsh, would have to raise interest rates into a war-driven price shock. On Friday the labor market handed that fear a counter-argument. The government reported that employers cut 23,000 jobs in July — a contraction, not a slowdown — and stocks rose. The S&P 500 added 0.6% to a record 7,757.64, the Nasdaq jumped 1.3% to 26,690.62, and the Dow rose 152 points to 54,036.93.

The logic is straightforward, if uncomfortable. A weaker jobs market cools the demand side of inflation and, crucially, gives the Fed cover to wait rather than tighten. Bonds agreed: the 10-year Treasury yield fell to 4.64% as traders trimmed the odds of a near-term hike. For a market that has spent the summer terrified of a hawkish central bank, a soft payrolls number read as relief.

How the week actually traded

It was a five-session sprint from record to record, with a mid-week wobble courtesy of oil. The Dow opened the month at an all-time high, then cleared 54,000 for the first time ever on Tuesday as Palantir and Caterpillar led an earnings-fueled surge. Wednesday and Thursday brought profit-taking and an oil scare; Friday's jobs report reset the narrative.

For the week the S&P gained 3.6%, the Dow rose 3.0%, and the Nasdaq outperformed as technology reclaimed leadership. The Russell 2000 finished the week just above 3,034, holding onto its position as the year's quiet leader.

Nvidia's week, and Musk's endorsement

The single biggest tailwind for tech came from Nvidia, up more than 10% on the week. The spark: on SpaceX's first earnings call since going public, Elon Musk said the company would build “exclusively on Nvidia,” citing the new Vera Rubin architecture as the best available for its AI ambitions. In a market newly skeptical of who actually profits from the AI build-out, a marquee customer naming its supplier out loud is worth a great deal.

A market that rallies on a shrinking payroll is not celebrating a weak economy. It is celebrating a patient Fed.The Friday Recap

What to watch next week

The relief rally now has to survive an inflation print. July's consumer price index lands mid-week, and it is the other half of the Fed's ledger. If prices ran hot while jobs cooled, Warsh's committee is squeezed from both sides — and this week's easy read on rates gets harder in a hurry. Oil remains the wild card: a genuine Hormuz agreement would take pressure off inflation and yields at once, while a collapse in talks would do the opposite.

The bottom line

Records across the board, powered by the paradox of a labor market weak enough to keep the Fed on hold. It works as long as inflation cooperates. Next week's CPI, and the state of the Hormuz talks, will decide whether this was the start of a new leg higher or a relief rally living on borrowed time.
