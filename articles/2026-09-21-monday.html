After the Hike: Trading the New Normal

The Fed has moved, the dot plot is hawkish, and the 10-year sits at 5%. With no FOMC to hide behind this week, the market has to decide for itself whether earnings can outrun the discount rate. Here’s the map.

JTM
James Thomas Minnehan
Senior Editor
MON · SEP 21 · 2026

Last week the market got the thing it had feared for months — the first rate hike in three years — and discovered it could survive it. The S&P finished the whipsaw roughly unchanged, the Nasdaq actually gained, and the fear gauge collapsed into Thursday’s relief rally. But surviving the event is not the same as resolving the question it raised. This week there is no FOMC meeting to circle, no single catalyst to wait for. That is precisely what makes it interesting: the market has to trade on its own read of a world it just learned is different.

We enter the week with the funds rate at 3.75%–4.00%, a dot plot in which 16 of 19 officials see more hikes coming, and the 10-year Treasury sitting at 5%. The soft-landing reflex is gone. Here is how I’m framing the days ahead.

1. Can the 10-year hold 5% without breaking stocks?

This is the single most important question of the week, and it has nothing to do with equities directly. The 10-year yield at 5% is the discount rate for the entire market and the benchmark for mortgages and corporate credit. Last week stocks proved they could tolerate a touch of 5%. What they haven’t tested is 5% as a floor that keeps grinding higher. Watch the long end of the curve every morning: if yields stabilize or ease, the relief rally has room to extend; if they push toward 5.1%–5.2% on fresh oil or supply concerns, the pressure on growth multiples resumes regardless of how good the earnings are.

2. Does the AI complex keep separating demand from the debate?

The most encouraging tell of last week was quiet: even amid an AI-safety scare that began with Dario Amodei’s call to slow the industry down, the semiconductor index finished the week slightly higher. Investors are drawing a line between the long-run governance debate and the very real near-term demand for chips, compute and data-center capacity. If that separation holds — if Nvidia and the AI infrastructure names can trade on order books rather than op-eds — the market keeps its most important engine. If a fresh safety headline reignites the “demand curve has a governor” fear, the chips are where the damage shows up first.

3. The geopolitical tape is now a market variable

Do not treat the Middle East as background noise this week. The oil complex is the transmission belt from geopolitics to the Fed to your portfolio, and last week the fuse got longer, not shorter — a potential $24 billion F-35 sale to Saudi Arabia, continued Houthi attacks, and the President openly weighing a “big decision” on Iran. Any of these can spike Brent overnight, and a Brent spike is now functionally a hawkish surprise: it lifts headline inflation, pressures the 10-year, and forces the Fed to stay restrictive. Keep a crude chart open next to your equity screens.

4. What Fed speakers say now that they've moved

The FOMC just delivered a unanimous hike and a hawkish set of projections, and this week the individual officials return to the podium. Their job will be to defend the trajectory Warsh laid out — that inflation is still too high and the economy is strong enough to take more tightening. Listen for whether they reinforce the “more to come” message or start hedging it. Any dovish crack from a voting member would be read as an early hint that Wednesday’s hawkishness was the peak, and the rate-sensitive corners of the market — small caps, housing, long-duration tech — would move first on it.

The setup in one line

The market enters the week resilient but re-priced: up double digits on the year, only about 2% below its record, yet operating under a discount rate it hasn’t had to respect in years. This is a “prove it” tape. The bulls need earnings, stable yields and calm in the Gulf to extend the Thursday bounce. The bears need only a 5%-plus 10-year or a fresh oil spike to remind everyone that the Fed put is retired. Trade the levels, respect the crude chart, and let breadth — not the headline index — tell you who’s winning.

The outlook

A quieter calendar, a louder question. With the hike behind us and no FOMC this week, the market trades on its own read of a 5% world. The four things that matter: whether the 10-year holds 5% or breaks higher, whether the AI complex keeps trading demand over debate, whether the Gulf stays quiet enough to keep Brent contained, and whether Fed speakers defend or soften Warsh’s hawkish line. Resilient market, higher bar. This is a week to trade breadth and levels, not headlines.
