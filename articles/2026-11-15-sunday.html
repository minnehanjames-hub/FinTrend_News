Sunday Macro Note

Inflation Is Spreading Past Oil. The Bond Market Has Noticed.

Producer prices rose 6.5% from a year earlier, the most since 2022, with food and freight doing the work energy did in September. The 10-year yield ended at 5.15% and a December hike is fully priced, while Nvidia’s leadership crisis showed how much of the market rests on one stock.

AM
Arshia Morshedian
Macro Strategist
SUN · NOV 15 · 2026

A month ago the inflation problem was oil. This week’s data said it is spreading. Consumer prices rose 0.4% in October and core prices 0.3% for a third straight month. Producer prices rose 6.5% from a year earlier, the most since November 2022, in a month when energy prices actually slipped. Food, freight and services did the lifting. The bond market responded: the 10-year Treasury yield rose 29 basis points on the week to 5.15%, and futures fully price a Fed hike on Dec. 9. Stocks rose anyway, with the S&P 500 up 2.5%. But the week’s other macro story, Jensen Huang’s resignation from Nvidia, was a reminder of how much of the market’s risk now sits in a handful of AI names.

1. Inflation moved past energy

The October producer price report is the clearest sign yet that the war’s energy shock is turning into broader inflation. Headline producer prices rose 0.3%, a little below forecasts, but the details were worse than the headline. Energy prices fell 0.1% and diesel 7.9%. At the same time, truck freight rose 3.9%, food 1.3% and basic organic chemicals 3.9%. Prices for services used as business inputs were up 6.8% from a year earlier, the most since November 2022. Excluding food, energy and trade services, producer prices rose 0.5% for a second straight month and 4.9% from a year earlier.

That is the sequence central banks worry about. Diesel spiked after the war began, trucking firms passed it on, and the cost is now showing up in groceries and services even as crude retreats. Tuesday’s consumer report pointed the same way: energy rose 0.7% in October, but food rose 0.5%, shelter 0.3% and motor vehicle insurance 0.7%. The Labor Department did not publish an annual CPI rate because it collected no prices in October 2025 during the government shutdown, so the monthly figures carry the signal. Three straight months of 0.3% core inflation annualize to roughly 3.7%, well above the Fed’s 2% target.

September’s inflation came through the gas pump. October’s came through the truck, the grocery aisle and the service invoice. That is the kind the Fed cannot look through.Arshia Morshedian

2. The Fed is arguing about pace, not direction

Nobody on the committee is arguing against a December hike. The debate is about size and sequence. Governor Christopher Waller said before the CPI release that the Fed should hike in December even if inflation cooled and consider a half-point move if it did not. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, who has dissented three times this year in favor of tighter policy, argued for the opposite approach: smaller, more frequent increases. “I don’t think we’re behind the curve today,” she said. New York Fed President John Williams said the Fed has work to do to get inflation back to target, and Chicago’s Austan Goolsbee said inflation looks like it is moving the wrong way. On Friday, Boston’s Susan Collins said some additional tightening may soon be appropriate, and San Francisco’s Mary Daly said the Fed will in all likelihood need to raise rates.

Markets have taken the message. A quarter-point hike on Dec. 9 is fully priced, while the odds of a half-point move, about 12% after Tuesday’s CPI, had slipped to single digits by Friday. Hammack’s argument for frequency over size suggests the next question is January, not the size of December. The policy rate is 3.75% to 4.00% after the September hike. One more point from Vice Chair Philip Jefferson on Friday is worth holding onto: if AI lifts the economy’s growth rate, he said, interest rates could be higher over time even if inflation falls. That is the opposite of the productivity story that has supported stock valuations.

3. Rates: the 10-year is back above 5%

The 10-year Treasury yield touched about 5.17% on Friday before closing at 5.15%, a four-week high, up from 4.86% a week earlier. The two-year rose 14 basis points on Thursday alone, after Trump said he no longer needs a deal with Iran, and ended the week at 4.87%. Thursday’s 30-year auction cleared at 5.441%. Ten-year breakeven inflation rose to 2.27%, a one-week high, and the dollar index reached a 19-month high. The 10-year is still below October’s peak above 5.30%, but the decline that followed the election has been more than reversed.

Housing is where higher long rates bite first. Existing-home sales fell 5.5% in October to an annual rate of 3.75 million, the weakest since August 2010, and mortgage rates track the 10-year yield. The labor market, by contrast, is not cracking: initial jobless claims were 200,000 last week and continuing claims fell to 1.697 million. That combination of a frozen housing market, a firm labor market and broadening inflation leaves the Fed little room to stop at one more hike.

4. Nvidia and the concentration problem

Huang resigned on Thursday after reports of two relationships, one with a business executive whom U.S. intelligence reportedly concluded was gathering information for China. Chief financial officer Colette Kress is interim chief executive and Harvey C. Jones is the board’s first chair. The stock lost just under $600 billion of market value this week and ended about a third below its October record, at roughly 12 times expected earnings, its lowest multiple since 2012, according to LSEG data cited by Reuters.

For macro, Nvidia matters through three channels. The first is the index: one stock’s governance crisis moved the S&P 500 on Thursday and Friday even as most stocks rose. The second is investment: AI equipment and data centers have been among the largest contributors to U.S. growth this year, and Nvidia has financed part of that build-out through stakes in and commitments to its own customers. Bernstein’s Stacy Rasgon, who cut his price target in half, questioned how those investments and guarantees will fare. The third is policy. Bloomberg reported that the episode gives lawmakers ammunition to tighten chip export curbs days before Trump meets Chinese President Xi Jinping at the APEC summit in Shenzhen on Nov. 18 and 19. The trade truce between the two countries runs to January.

Nvidia reports on Tuesday. Management had guided to about $108 billion of third-quarter revenue and preliminary growth of about 70% next fiscal year. The numbers will probably be strong; the market will be listening for whether Kress can hold the customer relationships and the investment pipeline that Huang personally managed.

5. Oil: the surplus argument returns

Brent fell 2.5% this week to $95.84, a second straight weekly decline, even with Trump’s Thursday post and continued attacks on Gulf shipping. The International Energy Agency raised its supply estimates and now sees a surplus of about 2 million barrels a day this quarter, and U.S. crude and fuel inventories rose. The physical risks have not gone away: Verisk Maplecroft counted 36 attacks on commercial ships in October, the most since the war began, and U.S. forces shot down two Iranian drones aimed at a base in Bahrain on Friday. Brent was near $72 before the war began in late February. Cheaper crude will help November’s inflation numbers, but the next CPI report arrives on Dec. 10, the day after the Fed decides.

6. The consumer, and a crowded week

Households are absorbing higher prices with falling real incomes: after-tax income adjusted for inflation fell 0.3% in September. October retail sales arrive Tuesday, with Home Depot, Target, TJX, Lowe’s and Walmart reporting through Thursday, which should show whether spending is holding up outside gasoline. The University of Michigan’s final November sentiment survey comes Friday. The week also brings the Fed’s October minutes on Wednesday and Anthropic’s planned Nasdaq listing, with a range of $70 to $78 a share for 825 million shares, or as much as $64.4 billion, at a valuation of about $1.4 trillion at the top of the range. A deal that size, landing in a week of governance questions at Nvidia and a 5% 10-year yield, is a direct test of how much new AI paper the market can absorb.

What it means for a portfolio

This week moved the macro case further toward higher rates for longer. Broadening inflation, a firm labor market and a 10-year yield above 5% favor shorter-duration bonds over longer ones and make rate-sensitive areas such as housing and small, indebted companies harder to own. Real assets and companies that can pass on costs have the edge in this kind of inflation. Within equities, the Nvidia episode is a reminder that index exposure now carries single-stock risk; equal-weight and value tilts reduce it. Cheaper oil remains the main source of relief, and it will reach the inflation data only with a lag.
