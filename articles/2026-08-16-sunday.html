Wall Street's Record, Main Street's Retreat

The indices sit at all-time highs. The consumer who is supposed to power the economy underneath them is quietly pulling back. That gap — and the AI capex boom papering over it — is the real macro story of the summer.

Arshia MorshedianMacro — The Sunday Macro Note
Sun · Aug 16, 2026

There are two economies in the data right now, and they are drifting apart. One lives on the stock ticker, where the S&P 500 spent the week making records. The other lives at the checkout counter, where July retail sales fell 0.6% and consumer confidence just dropped to 51.0 — a level that, in any other year, would read as a recession warning. The distance between those two pictures is the most important thing in markets this Sunday.

The consumer is telling on itself

The details are worse than the headline. Sentiment slid from 55.2 to 51.0 in a single month. Year-ahead inflation expectations rose to 4.3% — far above the 3.4% that prevailed before the Iran war. And in the survey's most quietly alarming line, just 8% of consumers expect their income to outpace inflation over the coming year. That is not a confident household. It is one bracing for its purchasing power to keep eroding, and starting to spend accordingly.

Set that against a headline CPI still running at 3.4% — cooler than June, but nowhere near the Fed's 2% target — and you have the outline of a stagflation-lite problem: softening demand and sticky prices at the same time. It is the least comfortable backdrop a central bank can face, because the two halves of its mandate point in opposite directions.

The wall holding it all up

So what is keeping growth — and the market — aloft while the consumer retrenches? Increasingly, one answer: artificial-intelligence capital spending. The hyperscalers have issued more than $200 billion of investment-grade debt in 2026 alone, and AI-related financing now accounts for close to 40% of all long-duration high-grade corporate bond supply, by Apollo's estimate — with room, the firm reckons, for as much as $2 trillion more. That building boom is a genuine pillar of demand. It is also a concentrated one.

When one theme funds itself with $200 billion of new debt and holds up an entire economy, the question isn't whether it's real. It's what happens if it pauses.The Sunday Macro Note

This is the vulnerability beneath the record. Corporate AI spending is doing work the consumer used to do, and it is being financed with an extraordinary wave of long-dated borrowing — borrowing that competes directly with the U.S. Treasury for the same pool of duration-hungry buyers. That competition is already showing up at the long end of the curve, where yields have been grinding toward multi-decade highs. If the consumer keeps fading, the capex pillar has to hold. And if that pillar's financing gets more expensive, the whole structure gets harder to support.

What we're watching

The gap between a record market and a retreating consumer, and whether AI capex can keep bridging it. Watch the long end of the Treasury curve for the first sign of strain — it is where the consumer's weakness and the capex boom's financing needs collide. That collision, not the next CPI print, is the risk that matters most.
