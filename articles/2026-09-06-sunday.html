The Fed's Reckoning Arrives

A hawkish chair, a jobs market that just roared back, oil pushing toward $96, and a president demanding the exact opposite of what the data supports. In ten days the Fed decides — and every thread of this strange year runs through that room.

Arshia MorshedianMacro — The Sunday Macro Note
Sun · Sep 6, 2026

Every so often a year of loose threads pulls tight into a single knot. This is that moment. In a little over a week the Federal Reserve will meet, and the decision it faces has been building since the spring: whether to raise interest rates into an economy that is slowing but not stalling, with inflation that is sticky but not spiraling, a war that keeps re-pricing oil, and a president publicly demanding cuts. The reckoning the market spent all summer postponing has arrived.

The dovish case just collapsed

For months the argument against a hike rested on one fact: the labor market was weakening, and July's data even showed employers shedding jobs. Friday erased it. August payrolls came in at 162,000 — roughly triple expectations — and, crucially, the revisions turned July's contraction positive. The single strongest piece of evidence for a patient Fed was revised out of existence. The 2-year Treasury yield jumped to 4.37%, its highest since early 2025, and the odds of a September hike climbed toward 58%. The market is no longer debating whether the Fed might hike. It is bracing for it.

Three forces the Fed can't wish away

The case for tightening is now well-built — resilient jobs, stuck inflation, a chair who sees a “global investment surge” rather than a slowdown. But three complications hang over the room. Oil is the first: Brent near $96 on a re-heating conflict with Iran is a live inflation impulse the Fed cannot control and cannot ignore. The long end is the second: thirty-year yields remain near multi-decade highs, held there by $40 trillion of federal debt and a flood of AI-linked corporate issuance that no policy rate can offset — and the Treasury's own buybacks have proven a band-aid. Growth is the third: at 1.5%, the economy is cooling even as hiring holds, and a hike would lean directly against that.

The Fed spent this cycle fearing it would have to cut into a recession. It now faces the stranger risk: hiking into a slowdown, with a war raising prices and a president demanding the opposite.The Sunday Macro Note

And now, the politics

The final thread is the one that makes this unlike any recent Fed decision: independence itself is on the table. The White House has spent the weekend celebrating the strong jobs number while pressing loudly for lower rates — the precise opposite of what the data now argues for. Warsh, who has made central-bank independence a theme of his tenure, walks into the meeting with the case for a hike strengthening and the political cost of delivering one rising in equal measure. How he threads that needle will tell markets more about the next year than any single quarter-point move.

What we're watching

The Fed's meeting later this month is now the year's hinge. Watch three things into it: the 2-year yield for the market's hike conviction, Brent crude for the inflation impulse, and the long end for whether the bond market believes a hike would finally break the back of both inflation and growth. After a summer of postponement, the decision can't be deferred again.
