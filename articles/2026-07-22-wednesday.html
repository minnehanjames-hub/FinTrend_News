Oil Takes the Wheel

Brent hit a six-week high on the eleventh straight round of strikes, Treasury yields retested May levels, and the index went nowhere while the market underneath it rotated hard.

Hunter William Lang · Markets Correspondent
Wednesday, July 22, 2026

The S&P 500 finished Wednesday down 0.14%. The Dow moved six points. On the surface, one of the quietest sessions of the month. Underneath it, Brent crude gained better than 3% to a six-week high, the 10-year Treasury yield retested levels last seen when the war began, and the Russell 2000 fell nearly a full percent.

The index was flat. The market was not.

Oil took the wheel

Brent crude futures rose about 3.4% to settle at $94.07 a barrel, briefly topping $95 and reaching their highest level in more than a month. West Texas Intermediate climbed roughly 3% to close at $86.83.

The move followed the eleventh straight round of U.S. strikes against Iran. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Iran is not serious about talks, adding that if they are serious, the United States is serious, and if they are not, Washington will do what is necessary to protect its interests and those of its allies.

The 10-year Treasury yield rose to 4.659%, retesting the highs seen in May as the conflict began. That combination — crude in the mid-nineties and a long end backing up — is the specific pairing that does the most damage to small caps, which carry more floating-rate debt and less pricing power than the megacaps above them. The Russell's 0.92% decline against the S&P's 0.14% is the breadth signal of the day.

The S&P's flatness is a composition artifact. Energy and materials strength masked weakness in semiconductors, software and consumer discretionary.

Reading Wednesday's internals

The tape underneath

Strength in materials, energy and utilities offset weakness in consumer discretionary and REITs. The Nasdaq opened in negative territory and stayed there for most of the session on softness in semis, software and large-cap technology ahead of the evening's earnings.

Philip Morris International also rallied on stronger-than-expected spring profits. Airbus left full-year 2026 guidance unchanged while targeting adjusted EBIT of €12–13 billion in 2029; Deutsche Bank analyst Christophe Menard noted the mid-term guidance aligned with expectations and that the positive surprise was the buyback.

Amazon's cuts are the latest round since the company announced in January that it would let go roughly 16,000 corporate workers.

Tariffs, quietly

President Trump said generic drugs imported to the United States will face a 100% tariff rate in August 2028, rising to 200% a year later, framed as an effort to onshore production. Most U.S. prescriptions are filled with generics manufactured offshore. The dates are distant enough that the market largely shrugged; the pharmaceutical supply chain implications are not.

And then the main event

Alphabet and Tesla reported after the close on the largest day of the week by market capitalisation. Both drew an unfavourable initial reaction in after-hours trade, with the focus falling squarely on capital expenditure rather than the headline results.

Tesla CEO Elon Musk told analysts on the call that this is a massive capex year and that he is confident the investments will yield incredible returns — possibly the best capex returns the company has ever seen. That framing is precisely the one the market has spent three weeks refusing to accept from anybody.

Where we stand

  • Breadth is deteriorating faster than the index. Russell −0.92% against S&P −0.14% is the number to carry into Thursday.
  • Crude at $94 changes the FOMC conversation. Last week's disinflation case was built on energy falling. It is not falling now.
  • Energy strength is flattering the index. Without it, Wednesday looks materially worse.
  • Capex was the story, as expected. The market asked the hyperscalers for receipts and got another spending commitment instead.
